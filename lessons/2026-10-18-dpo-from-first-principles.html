Hook. You have thousands of pairs of computer-use trajectories on the same tasks, one that the verifier passed and one it failed. Can you get the policy that PPO-RLHF would have produced on these preferences without training a reward model, without sampling a single rollout during training, and with a loss as simple as logistic regression? DPO says yes, provided you believe one modeling assumption and one closed-form identity. Then a second surprise: when you run it, the dashboard can show the success trajectories' log-probability going down while the loss improves. By the end of this lesson both facts should feel inevitable.

1. Bradley–Terry: preferences as logistic regression on reward differences

Intuition first. A labeler (or a verifier, or you) sees two responses and picks one. Suppose each response has a latent "goodness" \(r(x,y)\) and the judgment is noisy. If the noise on each option is i.i.d. Gumbel, a standard random-utility result (not from the papers below) says the choice probability is a logistic function of the difference:

$$u_i = r(x,y_i)+\varepsilon_i,\ \ \varepsilon_i\overset{iid}{\sim}\text{Gumbel}(0,1)\ \Longrightarrow\ p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\frac{e^{r(x,y_1)}}{e^{r(x,y_1)}+e^{r(x,y_2)}}=\sigma\big(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\big).$$
where:
  • x = prompt; y1, y2 = two candidate responses
  • r(x,y) = reward: a hidden "quality score" of response y
  • ui = the noisy utility a rater perceives; εi = Gumbel noise, drawn independently (iid)
  • y1 ≻ y2 = "y1 is preferred"; σ(z) = 1/(1+e−z), the logistic (sigmoid) function

Intuition: Only the difference in reward matters for which answer a person picks, and the gap maps to a probability via a sigmoid.

Worked example: r(y1)=2, r(y2)=1 → p = σ(1) ≈ 0.73. Gap 3 → σ(3) ≈ 0.95. Adding 10 to both rewards changes nothing.

This is the Bradley–Terry (BT) model, Eq. 1 in the DPO paper. Fitting it by maximum likelihood on a dataset \(\mathcal D=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\}\) is binary classification with logit \(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\):

$$\mathcal L_R(r_\phi)=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\big].$$
where:
  • rφ = learned reward model with weights φ
  • 𝒟 = dataset of (prompt x, preferred yw, rejected yl) triples
  • ℒR = average negative log-probability that the model ranks the pair correctly

Intuition: Train the reward model as a binary classifier: the chosen answer should score higher.

Worked example: Gap r(yw)−r(yl) = 1 → loss = −log 0.73 ≈ 0.31; gap −1 (wrong order) → −log 0.27 ≈ 1.31.

The unidentifiable direction. Add any \(f(x)\) to the reward and every BT probability is unchanged. DPO formalizes this as reward equivalence classes (\(r\sim r'\iff r-r'=f(x)\)) and proves that the class determines both the preference distribution (Lemma 1) and the KL-regularized optimal policy (Lemma 2). InstructGPT handles the same degeneracy in practice by adding a bias so that labeler demonstrations score 0 on average before RL.
Rankings, not just pairs. InstructGPT had labelers rank \(K\) outputs per prompt and trained on all \(\binom K2\) induced pairs, with the loss normalized by \(1/\binom K2\). The design detail that matters: they put all pairs from one prompt into a single batch element, because shuffling correlated pairs across the epoch made the RM overfit. Plackett–Luce is the full-ranking generalization, and the DPO derivation goes through for it too (DPO App. A.3).

2. The KL-regularized objective and its closed-form optimum

Standard RLHF (Ziegler → Stiennon → InstructGPT, as written in DPO Eq. 3) maximizes reward while staying near a reference policy, usually the SFT model:

$$\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\ y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\big[r(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[\pi(y|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\big].$$
where:
  • π = the policy being optimized; πref = frozen reference policy (usually the SFT model)
  • β > 0 = KL strength: how costly it is to drift from πref
  • 𝔻KL[π‖πref] = KL divergence = Ey~π[log π(y)/πref(y)], ≥ 0, zero only when they match

Intuition: Get high reward but pay a price for straying far from the starting model.

Worked example: Two answers, πref = (0.5, 0.5), rewards (1, 0), β=1. Policy (0.9, 0.1): reward 0.9, KL = 0.9 ln1.8 + 0.1 ln0.2 ≈ 0.37 → objective 0.53. Policy (0.73, 0.27) scores 0.73 − 0.11 ≈ 0.62, better — the optimum isn't "always pick the best".

The KL term keeps the policy where the reward model is accurate and keeps it from collapsing onto a single high-reward answer. In PPO practice it is folded into the reward: \(r - \beta(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\text{ref}})\). InstructGPT applies it as a per-token penalty. For a sequence, the sum of per-token KLs along the sampled path is the sequence KL (DPO's reward–KL frontier experiments report exactly this sum).

Derivation A (Gibbs' inequality, as in DPO App. A.1)

Fix \(x\). Pull \(\beta\) out, flip the sign, and multiply and divide by a normalizer:

$$\begin{aligned} \max_\pi \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}\Big[r-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\Big] &=-\beta\,\min_\pi \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\tfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}-\tfrac1\beta r(x,y)\Big]\\ &=-\beta\,\min_\pi \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\tfrac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}-\log Z(x)\Big]\\ &=-\beta\,\min_\pi\Big[\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*\big)-\log Z(x)\Big],\qquad Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}. \end{aligned}$$
where:
  • Z(x) = Σy πref(y|x) er(x,y)/β = partition function: the normalizer that makes the target a probability distribution
  • the steps only rearrange logs: multiply and divide inside by Z(x)

Intuition: The KL-regularized objective is just "minimize KL to a reweighted reference distribution", minus a constant.

Worked example: Same 2-answer setup: Z = 0.5e1 + 0.5e0 ≈ 1.859.

\(Z(x)\) doesn't depend on \(\pi\), and KL ≥ 0 with equality iff the distributions match. So

$$\boxed{\ \pi^*(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big)\ }\qquad\text{and the optimal value is }\ \beta\log Z(x).$$
where:
  • π* = optimal policy; other symbols as above

Intuition: The best policy is the reference policy with each answer upweighted by ereward/β.

Worked example: π*(y1) = 0.5·e / 1.859 ≈ 0.73, π*(y2) ≈ 0.27 — the policy that scored best above. The optimal value β log Z = ln 1.859 ≈ 0.62 equals 0.73·1 − KL ≈ 0.73 − 0.11.

Derivation B (Lagrangian; same answer, shows uniqueness)

Maximize \(\sum_y\pi(y)\big[r(y)-\beta\log\pi(y)+\beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\big]+\lambda\big(\sum_y\pi(y)-1\big)\). Setting \(\partial/\partial\pi(y)=0\) gives \(r(y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}-\beta+\lambda=0\), so \(\pi(y)\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}\). The objective is strictly concave in \(\pi\) (the \(-\beta\sum\pi\log\pi\) term), so this stationary point is the unique maximizer. Plugging back in gives the value: \(\mathbb E_{\pi^*}[r]-\mathbb E_{\pi^*}[r-\beta\log Z]=\beta\log Z\), a soft-max of the reward under \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). DPO §5.2 reads this as the soft value function of the reference policy, and argues it is the term PPO has to estimate with a learned critic (high variance if you leave it out).

β → ∞: \(e^{r/\beta}\to1\), so \(\pi^*\to\pi_{\text{ref}}\). The reward is ignored.
β → 0: \(\pi^*\) concentrates on \(\arg\max_y r\) within the support of \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). A response with \(\pi_{\text{ref}}=0\) never gets mass at any β.
In between: \(\log\pi^*-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}=r/\beta-\log Z\). β is a temperature that converts reward units into log-probability units.

Why don't we just sample from \(\pi^*\)? \(Z(x)\) sums over all sequences, which is exponential in length. The DPO paper calls it expensive to estimate even when you have the MLE reward. The rest of the lesson is about never computing it.

3. Inverting the optimum: your policy is an implicit reward model

Take the log of the boxed equation and solve for \(r\):

$$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x).$$
where:
  • πr = the optimal policy for reward r (π* above)

Intuition: Flip the formula: any reward can be read off its optimal policy as β·log(policy/reference), up to a per-prompt constant.

Worked example: β=1: log(0.73/0.5) + ln1.859 ≈ 0.38 + 0.62 = 1.0 = r(y1) ✓; log(0.27/0.5)+0.62 ≈ −0.62+0.62 = 0 = r(y2) ✓.

This holds for any reward and its optimal policy. Now apply it to the true reward \(r^*\) and plug it into BT. BT only sees differences, and \(\beta\log Z(x)\) is the same for both responses to the same prompt, so it cancels:

$$p^*(y_w\succ y_l|x)=\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Big).$$
where:
  • p* = true preference probability; yw/yl = chosen/rejected

Intuition: Because only reward differences matter, the unknown Z(x) cancels and preferences can be written purely in terms of the policy.

Worked example: Plugging the numbers: σ(0.38 − (−0.62)) = σ(1) ≈ 0.73 — the same as the BT probability from the rewards directly.

Replace \(\pi^*\) with a parametric \(\pi_\theta\) and do maximum likelihood, exactly as for the reward model:

$$\boxed{\ \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\underbrace{\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}}_{\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)}-\underbrace{\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}}_{\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)}\Big)\Big]\ }$$
where:
  • πθ = the trainable policy; r̂θ(x,y) = β log πθ/πref = its implicit reward

Intuition: DPO is the reward-model classification loss with the reward replaced by the policy's own log-ratio — no separate reward model or RL loop.

Worked example: β=0.1, chosen log-ratio +2, rejected −2 → margin 0.1·4 = 0.4 → loss = −log σ(0.4) ≈ 0.51.

Why this is not a trick (DPO Thm. 1). One worry is that restricting rewards to the form \(\beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) loses expressivity. It doesn't. Every equivalence class contains exactly one member of that form: the representative \(r-\beta\log Z_r(x)\), i.e. the one whose induced \(\pi\) normalizes to 1. Since the BT likelihood and the optimal policy depend only on the class (Lemmas 1–2), fitting BT over this parameterization is fitting BT over all rewards, and its optimal policy is \(\pi_\theta\) itself. Azar et al. (IPO, Prop. 4) strengthen this: DPO and RLHF-with-the-BT-MLE-reward share optimal policies even when the true preferences are not BT, as long as a BT minimizer exists.

Precise hidden assumptions, each of which becomes a failure mode later: (i) a single \(x\) shared by both responses, which is what makes \(Z(x)\) cancel; (ii) the nonparametric optimum is what you get, which ignores parameter sharing (§4.3); (iii) a BT minimizer exists with finite rewards, which fails for deterministic preferences (§5, IPO); (iv) the "action" \(y\) is chosen as one bandit arm, which matters for multi-step environments (§7).

Implementation (adapted from DPO App. B, with an agent loss mask)

def seq_logp(model, batch):
    # batch.input_ids interleaves instruction, screenshot tokens, tool outputs, and assistant turns
    logits = model(batch.input_ids, pixel_values=batch.pixels).logits[:, :-1]
    tok_lp = logits.log_softmax(-1).gather(-1, batch.input_ids[:, 1:, None]).squeeze(-1)
    return (tok_lp * batch.loss_mask[:, 1:]).sum(-1)      # SUM over assistant (thought+action) tokens only

with torch.no_grad():                                     # pi_ref is frozen: can be precomputed offline
    ref_w, ref_l = seq_logp(ref, chosen), seq_logp(ref, rejected)
pi_w, pi_l = seq_logp(policy, chosen), seq_logp(policy, rejected)
rw, rl = beta * (pi_w - ref_w), beta * (pi_l - ref_l)      # implicit rewards r_hat
loss = -F.logsigmoid(rw - rl).mean()
log(reward_margin=(rw - rl).mean(), reward_acc=(rw > rl).float().mean(),
    chosen_logratio=(pi_w - ref_w).mean(), rejected_logratio=(pi_l - ref_l).mean())  # watch the sign of chosen!

4. The DPO gradient: what each update actually does

Let \(u=\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\). Since \(\frac{d}{du}[-\log\sigma(u)]=-(1-\sigma(u))=-\sigma(-u)\):

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb E\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)}_{\text{large when the implicit RM ranks the pair wrongly}}\Big(\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}_{\text{push chosen up}}-\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}_{\text{push rejected down}}\Big)\Big]$$
where:
  • σ(r̂(yl) − r̂(yw)) = weight: probability the implicit reward currently ranks the pair wrongly
  • ∇ log πθ(y|x) = direction that makes response y more likely

Intuition: Push chosen up and rejected down, mostly on the pairs the model still gets wrong.

Worked example: Margin 0.4 → weight σ(−0.4) ≈ 0.40. Margin 4 → weight ≈ 0.018: nearly solved pairs barely contribute.

and each sequence term is a sum over loss-masked tokens: \(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y|x)=\sum_{t\in\text{mask}}\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})\).

The weight is the whole story. Pairs the implicit reward already gets right (\(u\gg0\)) have weight ≈ 0. Pairs it gets wrong have weight ≈ 1. The DPO paper reports that dropping this weight (plain "unlikelihood": maximize \(\log\pi(y_w)\), minimize \(\log\pi(y_l)\)) made models degenerate into repeated tokens. Minimizing a log-likelihood without bound always has a degenerate solution, and the sigmoid weight is what turns the push off.
What β does. β appears twice. It is a gradient scale, and it sets how big a raw log-ratio margin \(\Delta=u/\beta\) has to be before the weight \(\sigma(-\beta\Delta)\) dies off. With small β, the loss is only satisfied once the policy has moved far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) in log space. With large β, a small move is enough. This matches the closed form, where small β means weak regularization.

4.3 Why both likelihoods fall: likelihood displacement

Razin et al. (2024) name the phenomenon in which the DPO loss goes down while \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) also goes down likelihood displacement. They show it is not caused by multi-sample conflicts, capacity limits, or SFT. It already happens on a single prompt with single-token responses. Their mechanism, in a model where hidden embeddings \(h\) and the unembeddings \(W\) are trainable (single-token case, Thm. 1–2):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\ln\pi(y^+|x)\ \text{is more negative the larger}\ \ \underbrace{\langle W_{y^+},W_{y^-}\rangle}_{\text{chosen/rejected similarity}}+\sum_{z\neq y^\pm}\pi(z|x)\,\langle W_z,\ W_{y^+}-W_{y^-}\rangle,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\ln\pi(z|x)\propto\langle W_z,\ W_{y^+}-W_{y^-}\rangle+\text{const}.$$
where:
  • t = training time; y+, y− = chosen / rejected response (single tokens in this analysis)
  • Wy = unembedding (output) vector of token y; ⟨·,·⟩ = dot product (similarity)
  • z = any other token; π(z|x) = its probability

Intuition: If chosen and rejected look alike to the model, pushing them apart mostly shifts probability onto other tokens, so even the chosen likelihood can fall.

Worked example: Chosen and rejected vectors nearly identical (⟨Wy+,Wy−⟩ large): the update direction Wy+−Wy− is small and roughly orthogonal to Wy+, so ln π(y+) gains little and can drop, while a token z aligned with that difference gains.

In words: the update moves shared parameters along the difference direction \(W_{y^+}-W_{y^-}\). If chosen and rejected are nearly the same vector, that difference is mostly orthogonal to \(W_{y^+}\), so it barely helps the chosen response. Whatever third response lines up with the difference collects the mass instead, and it can mean the opposite of the chosen one. Their headline example is that training to prefer No over Never can sharply raise Yes. For multi-token responses the same role is played by hidden-embedding similarity, which they summarize as the CHES score. High-CHES pairs drive displacement, and filtering them out fixed an unintended loss of refusals better than adding an SFT term did.

Contrast: with independent tabular logits (no parameter sharing), the gradient of \(\log\pi(A)-\log\pi(B)\) with respect to the logits is exactly \(e_A-e_B\), because the normalizer cancels in the difference. \(\pi(A)\) can then only go up. Razin et al. likewise prove that displacement cannot occur in a linear model with fixed hidden embeddings. Displacement is a property of shared representations, not of the DPO objective alone.

5. One more hidden assumption: offline data and deterministic preferences (IPO)

Azar et al. (2023) write both RLHF and DPO as special cases of \(\Psi\)PO, \(\max_\pi\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi,y'\sim\mu}[\Psi(p^*(y\succ y'))]-\tau\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), with \(\Psi(q)=\log\frac{q}{1-q}\) recovering the BT/DPO case. Their key observation: if \(y\) always beats \(y'\) (\(p^*=1\)), BT needs \(r(y)-r(y')\to\infty\), so \(\pi^*(y')=0\) for every τ. The KL regularizer gets weaker the more deterministic the preferences are. With finite data this is the common case: a pair seen once is "deterministic" in the empirical distribution. They argue RLHF is partly protected because a trained reward model is underfit (regularized, early-stopped), whereas DPO, by skipping the RM, also skips that implicit regularization. Their toy experiments show DPO driving a never-winning action to 0 and a dominant action to 1 regardless of τ.

$$\text{IPO (}\Psi=\text{identity), sampled loss:}\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{IPO}}=\mathbb E_{(y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\Big(\log\frac{\pi(y_w)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}{\pi(y_l)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\frac{1}{2\tau}\Big)^2\Big]$$
where:
  • τ > 0 = IPO regularization; 1/(2τ) = target margin
  • the log term = (log π(yw)/πref(yw)) − (log π(yl)/πref(yl)), the same margin Δ as DPO without β

Intuition: Instead of pushing the margin forever, aim for a fixed target and stop.

Worked example: τ=0.5 → target 1. Margin 0.5 → loss 0.25; margin 1 → loss 0 (no more gradient); margin 3 → loss 4 (pulled back).

IPO regresses the same log-ratio margin Δ to a finite target \(1/(2\tau)\) instead of pushing it to infinity. Once the target is hit, the gradient is zero, and τ really controls the distance to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). (Razin et al. find likelihood displacement under IPO too, so a bounded margin limits the drift but doesn't remove the shared-feature mechanism. Compare the IPO preset above.)

Distribution shift, concretely. The derivation makes \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) the policy you regularize toward and implicitly the one the comparisons are about. If your pairs came from some other model, \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) can assign them odd likelihoods and the implicit rewards are measured from the wrong baseline. DPO's own fix, used when no SFT model matching the data existed: first SFT on the chosen responses and use that as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). The general cure is to re-sample pairs from the current policy (iterative/on-policy DPO). That moves DPO toward the online column, and UI-TARS's online bootstrapping does this for GUI agents (§7).

A useful mental model (my synthesis). PPO and GRPO estimate \(\pi^*\) by sampling from the current policy and correcting with importance ratios. DPO fits \(\pi^*\)'s log-ratio to labels at fixed points. That makes it cheap and stable, but it says nothing about the probability of responses it never sees, and the shared-feature mechanism in §4.3 decides where that unseen mass goes.

7. Computer-use agents: what is "y", and which pairs to build

7.1 The response is a trajectory interleaved with observations

A trajectory is \(\tau=(o_1,a_1,o_2,a_2,\dots,o_T,a_T)\): screenshots or AX-trees \(o_t\) from the environment, and assistant turns \(a_t\) (thought + action tokens) from the policy. Its probability factorizes as \(p_\theta(\tau)=\prod_t\pi_\theta(a_t|h_t)\,P(o_{t+1}|h_t,a_t)\). The environment terms are the same under \(\pi_\theta\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), so they cancel in the log-ratio:

$$\log\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau)}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ \sum_{j\in a_t}\Big[\log\pi_\theta(a_{t,j}|h_t,a_{t,<j})-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_{t,j}|h_t,a_{t,<j})\Big].$$
where:
  • τ (here) = an agent trajectory; pθ(τ) = its probability under the policy
  • T = number of steps; ht = history/screenshot context at step t
  • at,j = j-th token of action t; at,<j = the action tokens before it

Intuition: A trajectory's log-ratio is the sum of per-token log-ratios over the agent's own output tokens only; screenshots are conditioning, not scored.

Worked example: 2 steps, 3 action tokens each, each token +0.01 nats vs reference → trajectory log-ratio 0.06.

That is the loss_mask in the pseudocode: only assistant tokens count. Screenshot tokens are conditioning. They're processed in prefill for both models but never scored. Scoring screenshot tokens would be wrong twice over: the policy doesn't generate them, and including them would add huge, irrelevant log-prob terms to both sides.

Where the derivation stops being exact (my analysis, not from the papers). §2 treats \(y\) as one arm of a bandit. For a deterministic environment, a trajectory is fully determined by the action sequence, so "trajectory = arm" holds and the closed form holds over action sequences. For a stochastic environment (pop-ups, load timing, network), the KL-regularized optimum over trajectories differs from the bandit formula, because the policy can't choose \(o_{t+1}\). Pairwise DPO on trajectories is then a principled-looking heuristic rather than an exact solution, and an unlucky observation can make a good policy's trajectory the "rejected" one. Step-level pairs on the same state sidestep this.

7.2 Trajectory-level vs step-level preferences

Trajectory-level (\(x\)=task, \(y_w\)=a passing episode, \(y_l\)=a failing one). Cheap to label: the verifier does it. But it inherits the outcome-reward credit problem from Oct 14. One scalar weight multiplies the gradient of every token in both episodes, so 25 shared-quality steps in the failing episode get pushed down along with the one bad click. The two episodes also visit different screens, so they don't even share the context after the first divergence.
Step-level (\(x\)=history up to state \(s_\tau\), \(y_w\)=a correct action \(a'_\tau\), \(y_l\)=the erroneous \(a_\tau\)). Both sequences share the prefix \(h_\tau\), so the whole history cancels except through conditioning. The gradient touches only the tokens of the two candidate actions, which is precise credit assignment for free. UI-TARS builds exactly these pairs. During online bootstrapping it collects error steps with corrected actions (reflection tuning). For SFT it trains with the loss only on the corrected step, and then runs DPO on (\(a'_\tau\) ≻ \(a_\tau\) | \(s_\tau\)) with the SFT model as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), so the model learns to avoid the error, not just to imitate the fix.

Step-level pairs need a state where you know both a bad and a good action: human correction, reflection, a process reward model (Oct 14), or a branched rollout from a saved VM snapshot. These are the expensive part. Trajectory pairs are cheap but noisy. A practical recipe is to use trajectory pairs where the episodes differ early, and step-level pairs at known error states.

7.3 Length effects: DPO's sum vs GSPO's mean

DPO's margin is an unnormalized sum over action tokens. Write each step's average per-token log-ratio as \(\bar\delta_k\) and its token count as \(|a_k|\):

$$\Delta=\rho_w-\rho_l=\sum_{k\in y_w}|a_k|\,\bar\delta_k-\sum_{k\in y_l}|a_k|\,\bar\delta_k .$$
where:
  • ρw, ρl = trajectory log-ratios of chosen and rejected
  • |ak| = tokens in step k; δ̄k = mean per-token log-ratio of step k

Intuition: DPO's margin is a sum, so longer trajectories can move it more with the same per-token change.

Worked example: Chosen: 12 steps × 30 tokens × 0.01 = 3.6; rejected: 3 steps × 30 × 0.01 = 0.9 → Δ = 2.7 even though every token moved equally.

Consequences: (a) a longer trajectory (more steps, longer thoughts, inline code) can move its \(\rho\) much further with the same per-token shift. For example, 12 steps × 30 tokens × 0.01 nats/token = 3.6 nats, which adds 0.36 to the margin at β = 0.1. (b) Pushing \(\rho_l\) down is easiest by lowering many tokens a little, so long rejected sequences give cheap margin. (c) In hybrid GUI+code agents, verbose code steps dominate the sum, which is the same token-weighting problem that step-balanced GSPO fixes for RL, but without GSPO's 1/|y| normalization. If your chosen/rejected lengths differ systematically (e.g., successful runs are shorter), the implicit reward partly learns length. Check length-matched reward accuracy, and consider per-step-normalized or step-level pairs.

7.4 GUI pairs are almost always high-similarity pairs

A corrected click and an erroneous click often differ in a few coordinate or element-ID tokens: click(512, 300) vs click(518, 302), or the same thought with a different final element. In Razin et al.'s terms this is the high-similarity (high-CHES) regime where likelihood displacement is strongest, and the displaced mass can go to a third action, such as a neighbouring element or a different action type. Practical guards: log the chosen log-ratio and the probability of the top non-pair action at held-out states; add an SFT/NLL term on chosen actions (UI-TARS already SFTs on corrected steps before DPO); filter or down-weight near-duplicate pairs; or reach for IPO/bounded-margin variants.

7.5 Systems notes (ties to Oct 16)

  • \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) log-probs for a fixed dataset are a single prefill pass per sequence. Precompute them once and store one float per sequence, and you don't need the reference model in GPU memory during training.
  • Step-level pairs share the long screenshot-heavy prefix \(h_\tau\). Run the prefix once and branch into both candidate actions (prefix/KV sharing), instead of paying the O(n²) attention twice.
  • Truncating history for memory changes \(x\). Do it identically for \(\pi_\theta\), \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), chosen and rejected, or \(Z(x)\) no longer cancels: the two sides are then conditioned on different prompts.

8. Misconceptions

  • "DPO doesn't use a reward model." It uses one. It is implicit: \(\beta\log\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the reward model, trained with exactly the BT loss. That is the point of the "your LM is secretly a reward model" framing.
  • "Lower DPO loss means higher likelihood of good responses." The loss sees only the margin. Both log-ratios can fall (§4.2, §4.3).
  • "β is the KL coefficient, so DPO enforces a KL budget." In the population optimum, yes. On finite, near-deterministic pairs, the implied optimum can push margins without bound regardless of β (IPO's argument). In practice β mostly sets the margin scale where the gradient weight dies off.
  • "\(Z(x)\) cancels, so the prompt doesn't matter." It cancels only between two responses to the same \(x\). Comparing implicit rewards across different prompts or different truncated contexts is meaningless.
  • "DPO can teach new behaviors like RL can." It reweights within the pairs. Nothing is sampled, and a response with zero reference probability can never be reached (§2, β → 0 column).
  • "Include screenshot tokens in the log-prob, they're part of the sequence." They're environment output. Their terms are policy-independent in the true likelihood and must be masked.

9. Key takeaways

  1. BT turns preferences into logistic regression on reward differences, so rewards are identified only up to \(f(x)\).
  2. The KL-regularized optimum is \(\pi^*\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}\) (Gibbs' inequality), with value \(\beta\log Z(x)\).
  3. Inverting gives \(r=\beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}+\beta\log Z(x)\). \(Z\) cancels in same-prompt comparisons, which is the whole trick. The DPO loss is the BT loss with that implicit reward plugged in.
  4. The gradient is weighted by \(\sigma(\hat r_l-\hat r_w)\): mis-ranked pairs get pushed, solved pairs are ignored, and without the weight training degenerates.
  5. Only the margin is constrained. With shared representations, similar chosen/rejected pairs displace mass to third responses, so chosen likelihood can fall. Tabular parameterizations can't do this.
  6. Deterministic preferences let DPO ignore β. IPO regresses the margin to a finite target instead.
  7. For agents, mask non-assistant tokens. Step-level pairs on a shared state give precise credit and remove the environment-stochasticity caveat. Watch length effects in the unnormalized sum and displacement on near-identical action pairs.
One-paragraph summary. Bradley–Terry models a preference as a sigmoid of a reward difference. Fitting it is the classic RLHF reward-model loss, and it can never identify a per-prompt offset. The KL-regularized RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum \(\pi^*=\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z\), which is useless to sample from directly because \(Z\) sums over all sequences. Inverting it shows that any policy defines an implicit reward \(\beta\log\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\) up to \(\beta\log Z(x)\). That term cancels when you compare two responses to the same prompt, so you can fit the policy to preferences directly with logistic regression. That is DPO. Its gradient pushes chosen up and rejected down in proportion to how wrongly the implicit reward ranks the pair, but it constrains only the margin. With shared representations, similar pairs can lower both likelihoods and leak mass to unlabeled responses, and with deterministic data the KL "budget" disappears (the problem IPO fixes). For computer-use agents, the log-ratio is a sum over masked action tokens only. Step-level pairs at a shared state (as in UI-TARS) give precise credit, while trajectory pairs reintroduce outcome-reward smearing, length bias, and a stochastic-environment approximation.
