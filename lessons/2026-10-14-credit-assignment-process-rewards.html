Temporal credit assignment: from REINFORCE to process rewards

  2. Sunday (screen grounding): why can a perfect local click still leave the task failed?
  3. OSWorld 2.0 grades ~27 weighted final-state checkpoints — what does that buy you that binary pass/fail does not?
Check yourself(1) Errors compound; state drifts; agents spend <7% of budget on recovery/repair. (2) Local action success ≠ task success — grounding gets the pixel right, but the trajectory can still miss constraints, mid-task updates, or verification. (3) Partial checkpoints give a denser signal about where progress stalled, not just whether the whole job finished. Today's lesson goes to first principles: why sparse terminal rewards make long trajectories nearly unlearnable, and how process/step rewards (and those checkpoints) reshape credit assignment.

1. Intuition first: who gets blamed at step 300?

Imagine a 300-step GUI episode. The agent clicks Save on the wrong spreadsheet at step 47, never notices, and at step 300 the grader says "fail." Under a sparse terminal reward — +1 if the final state passes, 0 otherwise — every action in the trajectory is multiplied by the same return. The wrong Save, the fifty correct clicks that followed, and the final verification skip all share identical credit (or blame). The learning signal cannot tell step 47 from step 280.

That is the temporal credit assignment problem (Sutton & Barto): given delayed reward, which past actions caused the outcome? For computer-use agents, the delay is extreme — reward arrives after hundreds of observe–act cycles — so the problem is not a footnote; it is why RL on long GUI trajectories is hard even when grounding is solved.

2. Formal MDP for computer-use

Cast a computer-use episode as an MDP ⟨𝒮, 𝒜, P, R, γ⟩:

  • State st: screenshot It plus (compressed) history — prior actions, ledger/constraints, tool results. Partial observability is real; we treat the agent's input as st.
  • Action at: click(x,y), type(text), scroll, hotkey, or a tool call (bash, browser, ASK_USER). Discrete or continuous depending on the interface.
  • Transition: the OS/UI updates; the next screenshot is stochastic (animations, network, other apps).
  • Reward (classic sparse setup): rt = 0 for t < T, and rT ∈ {0, 1} from a binary task grader. Optionally discount γ ∈ (0,1].

Policy πθ(a|s) is the VLM/LLM agent (parameters θ). Objective:

J(θ) = Eτ ~ πθ[ G0 ]   where   Gt = Σk=tT γk−t rk

With sparse terminal reward and γ≈1, Gt = rT for every t — a single Bernoulli that paints the whole trajectory.

3. REINFORCE: the gradient and why variance explodes

Williams (1992) showed that the policy gradient can be estimated from sampled trajectories without differentiating through the environment. Start from the likelihood-ratio identity:

∇θ J(θ) = Eτ[ ∇θ log pθ(τ) · G0 ]

For a Markov trajectory, log pθ(τ) = Σt log πθ(at|st) + terms independent of θ (dynamics). Pushing the return inside the sum (causality: only future rewards after t matter) gives the familiar form:

∇J(θ) = E[ Σt=0T ∇ log πθ(at|st) · Gt ]

Monte Carlo estimate from one episode: for each (st, at), add ∇ log π · Gt to the gradient accumulator.

Why variance explodes with horizon.
  • Under sparse rT ∈ {0,1} and γ=1, Gt = rT for all t. Every timestep's gradient is scaled by the same random bit.
  • A random policy on a hard task succeeds with probability p ≪ 1. Most rollouts contribute 0; rare successes contribute +1 everywhere. The estimator is high-variance Bernoulli noise.
  • Even with denser rewards, Var(Gt) grows with the number of future reward terms (roughly like horizon for undiscounted sums of comparable noise). Long episodes → noisier Gt → noisier policy updates.
  • Credit is smeared: a bad early action and a good late action get the same Gt, so the gradient cannot preferentially reinforce the right decisions.
Intuition for computer-use: if only 1 in 100 long rollouts succeed, REINFORCE waits for those rare +1s and then upweights every action in them — including the lucky mistakes that happened not to kill the episode.
Mini derivation: why a state-independent baseline does not bias the gradient E[∇ log πθ(a|s) · b(s)] = Σa π(a|s) ∇ log π(a|s) · b(s) = b(s) ∇ Σa π(a|s) = b(s) ∇(1) = 0. So subtracting any function of s alone (or a constant) leaves ∇J unbiased while typically cutting variance. That is the door to baselines and advantages below.

5. Baselines and advantage

Replace Gt with an advantage:

At = Gt − b(st)  ≈  Gt − Vπ(st)

Vπ(s) = E[Gt | st=s] is the expected return from s. Then At asks: did this action do better or worse than average for this state?

  • Unbiased: as shown above, any b(s) (or constant) has expectation zero against ∇ log π.
  • Lower variance: Gt and V(st) are correlated; subtracting removes shared noise. A good baseline is the biggest free lunch in policy gradients.
  • Still smeared under sparse rewards: if Gt = rT for all t, then At = rT − V(st). Early states with similar V still get nearly identical advantages when rT is the only signal — baselines help, but they do not invent intermediate credit.

Actor-critic trains a critic Vφ alongside the actor πθ, and uses Vφ(st) as the baseline (or bootstraps one-step TD: δt = rt + γ V(st+1) − V(st)).

6. GAE intuition (λ-return) — without a full PPO tutorial

Schulman et al. (2016) introduced Generalized Advantage Estimation. Define the TD residual δt = rt + γ V(st+1) − V(st). Then:

ÂtGAE(γ,λ) = Σl=0∞ (γλ)l δt+l
  • λ = 0: Ât = δt — pure 1-step TD. Low variance, high bias (depends heavily on V).
  • λ = 1: Ât = Gt − V(st) — Monte Carlo advantage. Low bias, high variance.
  • λ ∈ (0,1): exponential mix of n-step advantages — the bias–variance dial used in modern on-policy LLM-RL (PPO/GRPO-style trainers almost always sit on GAE or a close cousin).

For computer-use: GAE helps when you have some intermediate reward structure or a decent value model. It does not magically solve a 300-step episode with a single terminal bit — you still need denser targets or a critic that has learned long-horizon value (itself hard).

7. Process rewards vs outcome rewards

Outcome reward (ORM-style): grade only the final answer / final UI state. One scalar for the whole trajectory.
Process reward (PRM-style): grade intermediate steps — each reasoning step, or each GUI checkpoint.

Lightman et al. ("Let's Verify Step by Step", 2023) showed that process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision for training reward models on MATH: step-level labels give a denser, more local training signal and reduce "right answer, wrong reasoning." The same logic transfers to agents:

  • OSWorld 2.0's ~27 weighted final-state checkpoints are a form of process-shaped evaluation: they tell you which subgoals held, not just pass/fail. Used as training rewards (or as shaping), they turn one Bernoulli into a vector of subgoal successes.
  • Step-level / checkpoint rewards shrink the effective credit horizon: an action that completes "attach receipt" gets credit at that checkpoint, not only at episode end.
  • Trade-off: process rewards can be misspecified (reward hacking on the proxy), expensive to label, and may encourage myopic subgoal-chasing. Outcome rewards are aligned with the true goal but starve the learner of signal.

For GUI RL, practical densification patterns include: checkpoint matchers on DOM/file state, progress heuristics (form fields filled), PRM-style judges on action traces, and verification tools that emit intermediate ± scores.

9. Connecting the dots for computer-use training

Engineering synthesis — not a claim from any single paper.

  1. Sparse terminal RL alone is a poor fit for 300-step GUI tasks. REINFORCE variance and smeared credit make learning sample-hungry even when local grounding is good (Sunday's lesson).
  2. Baselines / GAE are necessary but not sufficient. They cut variance; they do not create intermediate targets. Pair them with denser rewards or a strong critic.
  3. Process rewards / checkpoints reshape the credit landscape — the same reason OSWorld 2.0's partial scoring reveals "last mile" failures that binary hides, and the same reason PRMs beat ORMs on multi-step math.
  4. Don't confuse eval checkpoints with reward shaping. Using the grader's internals as training reward can overfit to the benchmark; prefer independently specified subgoals or learned PRMs.
  5. Recovery behavior needs its own credit. Yesterday: agents spend <7% budget on repair. If only final success is rewarded, repair actions that don't change the terminal bit get no learning signal — consider explicit rewards (or preferences) for verified fixes.

10. Key takeaways

  1. Temporal credit assignment asks which past actions caused a delayed outcome — the central difficulty of RL on long computer-use trajectories.
  2. REINFORCE: ∇J = E[Σt ∇ log π(at|st) · Gt]. With sparse terminal reward, Gt is the same random bit for every t → high variance, smeared credit.
  3. Subtracting a state-value baseline does not bias the gradient; advantages At = Gt − V(st) cut variance.
  4. GAE(γ,λ) interpolates TD and Monte Carlo advantages — the bias–variance dial behind modern on-policy LLM-RL.
  5. Process/step rewards densify credit (PRMs; OSWorld-style checkpoints). They help learning but must be designed to avoid reward hacking.
  6. Local action success ≠ task success: grounding can be perfect while credit assignment still fails the long job.
